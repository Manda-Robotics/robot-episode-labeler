What we learned measuring a robot video annotator

An endpoint that turns a robot manipulation episode into timestamped subtasks. Every number below is measured on a public benchmark — including the two changes we expected to work, and didn't.

share of boundaries found within ±0.25s · ±0.5s · ±1s · ±2s · missed

The product

Robot teams sit on thousands of teleoperation episodes that are effectively opaque. To find every failed grasp of the yellow block, someone has to watch them. Making episodes temporally and semantically searchable is the useful part of what annotation vendors sell, and much of it can now be produced automatically.

So: send a video and a sentence describing it, get back structured labels. No onboarding, no ontology workshop, no annotation contract.

{ "start_seconds": 4.25, "end_seconds": 10.50,

  "attributes": ["retry"], "confidence": "low",
  "flags": ["boundary_moved_0.90s"] }

Deliberately not in scope: object masks, 6D pose, 3D reconstruction, dense captioning, or a human-in-the-loop review operation. The bottleneck in public benchmarks is temporal boundaries, so that is where the engineering went.

What we will not claim

These use our scoring protocol: greedy one-to-one matching at IoU ≥ 0.5, pooled across the corpus. The benchmark's authors report 0.306 segmentation F1 for their best configuration, but their published repository contains no scoring code, so we cannot know the protocols match. This is not a head-to-head win, and we do not present it as one.

First, the noise floor

Before trusting any improvement, we ran the identical configuration twice at temperature 0.

Temperature 0 is not determinism. On a 100-episode corpus that produces swings of ±0.03–0.08 in corpus F1 from nothing at all.

So the project adopted a rule: nothing counts as an improvement unless a paired bootstrap over episodes gives a confidence interval excluding zero, and the effect replicates against a second independent baseline run. The first casualty was one of our own claims — a “+0.024 F1” improvement that evaporated, and came out at −0.014 against the second baseline.

The finding that mattered

Recall is almost entirely a function of how long the event lasts. Missed events had a median duration of 2.4s; found ones 5.5s.

This is not primarily a reasoning failure. At 0.5s sampling with 20 frames per contact sheet, a one-second event is two frames inside a sheet spanning ten seconds — the model can barely see it. The benchmark's authors report the same shape (7.4% recall under two seconds), which suggests it is a property of the contact-sheet approach rather than of any particular model.

The fix follows directly: re-read any predicted segment longer than 3s at 0.25s sampling, and split it where a second completed event is visible. Inference is spent only where an event can hide.

It is the largest gain in the project, significant at every tolerance across three independent comparisons on two different models.

The honest trade

Subdivision buys recall with precision. On the weaker model, precision fell 0.635 → 0.480 and F1 did not move at all — we found many more real events and over-split some genuine ones. For making episodes searchable a missed event is worse than a spurious boundary a user can see and skip, so we took the trade, and low-confidence splits carry flags.

Two things that didn't work

Boundary refinement — the change we bet on

The plan's central bet was localised refinement: re-read a dense window around each proposed boundary and re-place it. The uncertainty is concentrated at boundaries, so it should pay.

Robot telemetry — the obvious edge

A gripper opening or closing is the world-state change that defines most manipulation boundaries, and the benchmark ships synchronised gripper channels. It looked like a free advantage over a video-only competitor. We measured it before building on it:

Strong on one robot, weak on another, absent on the family contributing 470 of the 743 gold segments. Since the product's premise is that you can send a plain video, the video-only path has to carry accuracy by itself. It stayed an optional enhancement rather than the foundation.

Both bets came from the plan. The change that actually worked came from measuring recall against event duration and noticing it was zero below one second. The measurement chose, not the plan.

What it still gets wrong

Events under one second are close to invisible — 0.038 recall. Timestamps have sub-second resolution; their accuracy is a much weaker claim, and we do not describe the system as sub-second accurate.

Label accuracy is uneven. 0.806 on Galaxea and 0.800 on HomER, but 0.461 on DROID. DROID's gold labels read like task instructions ("Move the silver cup to the right") rather than event descriptions, so part of that gap may be a scoring-convention mismatch rather than a model failure. Not yet investigated.

The corpus is small and unbalanced. 100 episodes, with HomER supplying 470 of 743 segments, so pooled metrics lean egocentric and per-family numbers from single runs are noisier than the corpus. We report both.

Where it runs

One pipeline, three entry points: a Python API, a CLI, and a container. Output as JSON, JSONL, CSV, or MCAP — episode-relative segment times on an absolute log clock, so annotations drop into a team's existing tooling instead of arriving as another sidecar format.

Two engineering notes worth passing on, both found by running the thing rather than reading it. Homebrew's macOS ffmpeg ships an AV1 decoder that is videotoolbox-only: on hardware without AV1 acceleration, every AV1 video is silently undecodable — a quarter of the benchmark, and any customer sending AV1. And a request with no deadline will wedge every worker at 0% CPU indefinitely. Decoding is now a property of the package rather than of the host, and every request has a timeout.
